From Sunlight to Turbines: Exploring Energy Generation with Nadiyah

A conversation about windmills, water propellers, lightning, fission, and fusion became a lesson in the systems engineering of electricity: where energy begins, how it changes form, and why conversion matters as much as the source.

Published August 2, 2026

KEY POINTS

  • Electricity is usually an energy carrier rather than the original energy source.
  • Most electricity reaches the grid through one of two major conversion paths: electromagnetic generators driven by motion, or photovoltaic cells driven directly by light.
  • Fission and fusion are nuclear energy sources, but both can feed a familiar thermal-mechanical chain: heat, fluid motion, turbine rotation, and electrical generation.
  • Lightning contains real electrical energy, but its extreme power, short duration, unpredictability, and storage requirements make it a poor practical resource.
  • The strongest mentoring moments begin with the learner's model and improve it, rather than replacing it with a memorized list.

Starting With Her Model

I recently asked my daughter Nadiyah how people generate the electricity we use. She identified windmills, water "propellers," lightning, and mechanical movement. That is a remarkably useful starting model. Wind turbines and hydroelectric turbines really do convert moving fluids into rotation, while lightning is undeniably electrical. Her answer already contained the central ingredients of the problem: a source of energy, a physical process, and an electrical result.

Instead of treating the answer as a list to be corrected, we treated it as an engineering model to refine. The first refinement was vocabulary. The devices in hydroelectric plants are turbines rather than ordinary propellers. A propeller generally uses rotation to push a fluid; a turbine works in the reverse direction, extracting energy from moving water, steam, or gas to create rotation. Both rely on similar fluid-mechanical principles, so her intuition was sound.

Electricity Is the Destination, Not Usually the Starting Point

The most important insight in our discussion was that electricity is generally not a primary energy source. It is an energy carrier: a convenient form into which other energy is converted, transported through wires, and converted again into light, heat, computation, sound, or motion. A power plant does not create energy from nothing. It changes energy from one form to another while losing some of it to unavoidable waste heat, friction, electrical resistance, and auxiliary equipment.

At the grid level, most generating technologies therefore reduce to two dominant electrical conversion mechanisms. The first is the electromagnetic generator. Mechanical rotation changes the magnetic flux through conductors, inducing voltage and current. Wind turbines, hydroelectric plants, steam plants, gas turbines, and many engine-generators all use this principle. The second is the photovoltaic cell, in which photons transfer energy to electrons in a semiconductor and produce electrical current without a rotating generator.

THE BASIC SYSTEM MODEL

Primary source → conversion process → mechanical motion or direct charge movement → conditioned electricity → useful work

Solar and Mechanical: A Good Framework With One Refinement

I broke the subject into solar and mechanical generation for Nadiyah. Pedagogically, that works well because it highlights the two conversion paths that are easiest to see: sunlight acting directly on a solar cell, and motion turning a generator. Technically, however, solar and mechanical belong to different levels of the system. Solar radiation is a source; mechanical motion is normally an intermediate form of energy. A more complete framework separates where energy originates from how it is converted into electricity.

The distinction reveals an interesting family tree. Sunlight heats Earth's surface unevenly, which helps produce wind. Wind can create ocean waves. Solar heating also drives evaporation and the water cycle, raising water that may later descend through a hydroelectric turbine. Plants store solar energy through photosynthesis, and fossil fuels contain chemical energy inherited from ancient life. Thus wind, conventional hydropower, biomass, and fossil fuels are all indirect descendants of solar energy, even though most produce electricity mechanically.

Not every source belongs to that solar family. Geothermal energy comes from heat within Earth, including radioactive decay and residual planetary heat. Tidal energy is primarily gravitational, driven by the Earth-Moon-Sun system. Nuclear energy comes from changes in atomic nuclei. Chemical batteries move charge through electrochemical reactions and deliver electricity without first spinning a turbine.

Fission and Fusion: Opposite Reactions, Similar Power-Plant Logic

We also discussed fission and fusion. In fission, a heavy nucleus such as uranium splits into smaller nuclei after interacting with a neutron. The reaction releases energy, radiation, and additional neutrons that can sustain a controlled chain reaction. Existing nuclear power plants capture the released heat, use it to produce steam, and use that steam to turn a turbine-generator. At the electrical end of the plant, fission power is therefore still mechanical generation.

In fusion, light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus. The resulting products have slightly less mass than the original particles; that mass difference appears as energy according to E = mc2. Fusion powers the Sun and stars. Practical terrestrial fusion systems must first create and confine an extremely hot plasma, then capture the released energy while protecting structures from intense heat and radiation. Many proposed fusion plants would transfer that energy into a coolant or blanket and ultimately run a turbine-generator, although direct energy conversion remains an important research topic for some fusion approaches.

This leads to a useful comparison: fission and fusion are dramatically different at the nuclear and reactor levels, but a future fusion facility and a present fission plant may look surprisingly familiar downstream. Both can follow the chain nuclear binding energy → heat → fluid motion → turbine rotation → electricity. Inside the generator, the turbine rotates magnets relative to coils of conductive wire. The changing magnetic field produces voltage and drives electric current through the wire—a process called electromagnetic induction. Michael Faraday discovered this principle in 1831, and it remains the foundation of most large electrical generators today.[1]

Could Lightning Be Captured?

Nadiyah's lightning proposal deserves a serious answer. Lightning is a rapid atmospheric electrical discharge produced when separated charges create an electric field strong enough to overcome air's normal resistance to electrical flow. NOAA reports that an average lightning bolt carries approximately 30,000 amperes of current and has an electrical potential of roughly 300 million volts, although actual flashes vary substantially.[2] In less technical terms, voltage describes the electrical "push," while current describes how much electric charge is flowing. Lightning has enormous amounts of both—but only for a very short time, through an unpredictable path. That combination makes its energy extremely difficult to intercept, convert, and store safely. A spectacular peak lasting only a short time is not automatically a large, steady fuel supply.

A conceptual harvesting system would need a tall strike receptor, a very low-impedance path, surge protection, pulse-forming networks, and a first-stage storage system able to accept energy far faster than an ordinary battery. Supercapacitors or specialized pulse-power equipment might absorb and stretch the event before converters transferred energy into longer-duration storage or the grid. The control system would also have to isolate faults, verify grounding, coordinate switching, and prevent a failed capture device from turning a protection tower into a hazard.

The obstacles are decisive. Strikes are intermittent and geographically unpredictable. The voltage can arc across or through equipment rather than follow an intended circuit. Conductors experience extreme electromagnetic and thermal stress. Batteries cannot directly accept such short, intense pulses. Much of the discharge energy heats and ionizes the air, produces shock waves and light, or dissipates through the ground. A collection station would therefore require expensive equipment that sits idle most of the time and may be damaged by the very event it is meant to harvest.

Conventional lightning rods illustrate the engineering priority: they do not store a strike; they provide a controlled path to ground so that buildings and people are protected. It is theoretically possible to divert and condition part of a lightning discharge, but current economics and materials make wind, solar, hydroelectric, geothermal, and nuclear systems far more practical. A subtler research question may be whether atmospheric charge can be collected gradually before a full discharge forms. Gradual collection would reduce the pulse-power problem, but the available current density, weather dependence, scale, and safety requirements remain formidable.

Generation Is Really a Systems-Engineering Problem

Our discussion began with sources, but a reliable electrical system requires much more than a source. Engineers must control voltage, frequency, phase, protection, grounding, thermal limits, storage, transmission, and demand. Solar panels require inverters and often storage. Wind turbines require pitch control, power electronics, braking, and grid synchronization. Nuclear plants require multiple layers of reactor control, cooling, monitoring, and independent protection. Hydroelectric stations manage gates, reservoir conditions, rotating machinery, and environmental constraints.

This is why efficiency alone cannot select an energy technology. Engineers also evaluate capacity factor, dispatchability, geographic availability, construction cost, fuel supply, waste streams, land and water use, maintainability, safety, and resilience. A diverse grid combines resources with different strengths rather than searching for one perfect machine.

The Mentorship Insight

The best part of the conversation was not correcting "water propellers" to turbines. It was watching Nadiyah identify motion and electrical charge as common threads across systems that initially look unrelated. That instinct—searching for a shared mechanism—is exactly what engineers do when they construct a useful abstraction.

A productive science conversation with a young person does not need to begin with the official taxonomy. It can begin with her own model, preserve what is physically sound, expose where categories are being mixed, and then rebuild the model with clearer boundaries. Nadiyah's original division was not wrong; it was one layer short. Solar describes an origin, mechanical describes a conversion state, and electricity describes a transportable output. Adding those layers transformed a list of machines into a system.

MENTORSHIP

Curiosity supplied the components. Systems thinking connected them: identify the source, follow every conversion, and ask what must be controlled before the output becomes useful.
